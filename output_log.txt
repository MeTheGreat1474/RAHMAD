      KUALA LUMPUR: The High Court has ordered the government to pay RM195,000 in damages to 13 participants of a 2021 candlelight vigil at Dataran Merdeka after finding it vicariously liable for their unlawful arrest and detention by police.
      Judge Datuk Anand Ponnudurai awarded each plaintiff RM15,000 in general damages.
      He declared that the plaintiffs' arrest and detention on Aug 19, 2021, were unlawful and breached their right to personal liberty under Articles 5(1) and 5(3) of the Federal Constitution.
      However, Anand dismissed their claims under Article 10(1)(b) of the Federal Constitution, which concerns the right to peaceful assembly without arms.
      He also rejected their claims for aggravated and exemplary damages, as well as misfeasance in public office.
      The plaintiffs attended the vigil while measures under the Covid-19 Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases legislation were in force.
      They alleged that they were unlawfully arrested, wrongfully detained for four hours, assaulted and battered, denied access to legal representation, had their constitutional rights infringed, and that police had committed misfeasance in public office.
      The 27 defendants, 23 of whom are policemen and officers, denied the allegations, contending that they had lawfully discharged their statutory duties in monitoring compliance with the prevailing public health regulations.
      They said the plaintiffs repeatedly refused lawful directions to disperse and subsequently refused to cooperate with police by proceeding to the police station.
      They maintained that any physical contact with certain plaintiffs involved no more than reasonable force and denied that the plaintiffs were deprived of legal representation.
      Anand ruled that the plaintiffs were deprived of their liberty when they were arrested and compelled to go with police from Dataran Merdeka to the station.
      "A reasonable person placed in the position of the plaintiffs would not have believed that he or she remained free to disregard the police instructions and simply walk away.
      "Their identity cards had been retained. Their requests to travel independently had been refused.
      "They were required to board police vehicles. Those who initially refused were compelled to do so.
      "Upon arrival at the Dang Wangi police headquarters, they remained under police control until their eventual release.
      "Those circumstances admit only one realistic conclusion.
      "Whatever terminology may have been employed by the police officers at the scene, the plaintiffs had, by that stage, been deprived of their liberty in a manner amounting to an arrest in law.
      "There was no warrant in this case. Accordingly, the court finds that the arrests of the plaintiffs were unlawful," he said.
      Anand found that the plaintiffs had established a breach of their constitutional rights under Article 5(3) as they were denied reasonable and timely access to legal representation while being detained at the police station.
      He said that although lawyers were eventually granted access, many statements had already been recorded and the plaintiffs had not been afforded a meaningful opportunity to consult their lawyers, who were permitted only to observe the recording of the statements.
      He also found that the plaintiffs had established battery only in respect of the intentional application of force during the unlawful arrests and the marking of their forearms, to identify their seized belongings, without consent.

      He added that the plaintiffs were not subjected to prolonged incarceration, oppressive interrogation or physical abuse, and rejected the allegation that excessive force was used.
      He further found that police were engaged in a genuine law enforcement operation during an unprecedented public health emergency.
      "While the officers exceeded the legal limits of their powers, I have accepted that they acted in the honest belief that their conduct was authorised by law.
      "There is no evidence of malice, spite, targeted hostility or any deliberate abuse of public office," he said.
